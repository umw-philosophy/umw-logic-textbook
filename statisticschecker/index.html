Worked solution

    Reference

    Mean, median, and mode
    • Mean: add the values and divide by how many there are.
    • Median: put the values in order and take the middle one. With an even number of values, average the two middle values.
    • Mode: the value that appears most often. One mode is unimodal; two is bimodal. If every value appears once, there is no mode.
    Choosing a measure
    • Mean: the data is fairly symmetric with no extreme outliers.
    • Median: the data is skewed, or a few extreme values pull the mean away from where most of the data sits.
    • Mode: the question is about which value comes up most often.
    • A mean well above the median suggests a long tail of high values (skewed right). A mean below the median suggests a tail of low values (skewed left).
    Range and standard deviation
    • Range = largest value − smallest value.
    • Sample standard deviation, in six steps:
      1. Calculate the mean.
      2. For each value, find value − mean.
      3. Square each of those differences.
      4. Add up the squared differences.
      5. Divide by one less than the number of values. This is the variance.
      6. Take the square root. This is the standard deviation.
    Standard deviations from the mean
    • Subtract the mean from the value, then divide by the standard deviation.
    • A value 2 standard deviations above the mean is high; 2 below is low; within 1 is in the middle of the pack.
    The empirical rule (68-95-99.7)
    • About 68% of values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean, 95% within 2, and 99.7% within 3.
    • One tail is half of what lies outside a band: about 16% beyond 1 standard deviation on one side, 2.5% beyond 2, and 0.15% beyond 3.
    • A value more than 2 standard deviations from the mean is unusual; more than 3 is extremely unusual.
    Margin of error (rule of thumb)

    At the 95% confidence level:

    Sample size and margin of error
    Sample sizeMargin of error
    400± 4.9%
    500± 4.4%
    600± 4.0%
    1,000± 3.1%
    1,500± 2.5%
    2,000± 2.2%
    • The interval runs from the estimate minus the margin to the estimate plus the margin.
    • A majority is shown only if the whole interval lies above 50%.
    • Two results (two candidates, or two polls) differ by more than chance only if their intervals do not overlap.
    • The margin of error is half the interval's width.
    Sensitivity and specificity
    • Sensitivity: how often the test is positive when the condition is present. False-negative rate = 1 − sensitivity.
    • Specificity: how often the test is negative when the condition is absent. False-positive rate = 1 − specificity.
    • Flagging more people raises sensitivity and lowers specificity; flagging fewer does the reverse.
    Thresholds and cutoffs
    • A threshold of k standard deviations flags readings below mean − k × SD or above mean + k × SD.
    • Healthy readings flagged (false positives): about 32% at 1 SD, 5% at 2 SD, 0.3% at 3 SD. Those are both tails together.
    • A wider normal range flags fewer readings: fewer false positives, more false negatives. A narrower range does the reverse.
    Distortions
    • Percent difference: (larger − smaller) ÷ the value you compare against × 100.
    • Truncated axis: each bar's visible height is its value minus where the axis starts.
    • A picture scaled k times taller (and wider) covers k × k times the area.
    • Absolute risk reduction: control rate − treatment rate, in percentage points. Relative risk reduction: that difference ÷ the control rate × 100.
    • Percent change from a starting year: (end − start) ÷ start × 100.
    Spreadsheet functions
    • =AVERAGE() gives the mean; =MEDIAN() the median.
    • =STDEV.S() gives the sample standard deviation (divides by n − 1).
    • =STDEV.P() gives the population standard deviation (divides by n).
